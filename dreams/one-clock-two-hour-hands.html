One Clock, Two Hour Hands

← Back to the gallery

🔴 pure daft, a night train up a snowbound mountain, and two of the oldest bugs in the book.

The dream

There's a narrow-gauge railway that climbs a mountain at night, in the snow — a real train, a little steam locomotive and a handful of carriages — and it doesn't go up so much as up and across at once. The mountain's long slope rises from east to west, and the line climbs it heading west: every halt further along the line is both a little higher and a little further west than the one below it. (A halt, if the word's new to you, is the smallest kind of railway stop — a bare platform, no station, sometimes just a post and a nameboard in the snow.)

Which is why every halt on it keeps its own time. Not out of stubbornness — out of the sun. Years back, each halt set its clock by the moment noon actually landed on its own platform. And because the Earth turns west to east, the sun reaches noon a few minutes later at every point further west — every halt further up the line. So each little platform's clock runs a few minutes behind the one below it, the whole way down to the valley, and every single one of them is right, for exactly where it stands. (This is real history, by the way — it's precisely why railways had to invent standard time. You cannot run a timetable when every town down the line keeps its own noon.)

The train's engine — the locomotive, the steam engine hauling the whole lot up the hill — has a clock too. Brass, bolted to the boiler, and nobody has ever set it. It left the works reading the works' time and no one's touched it since. It isn't wrong. It just isn't anywhere. It keeps one time, the same time, the entire way up the mountain, whichever halt it happens to be sitting at.

The driver drives. I keep the logbook. That's the train — and that's the two of us.

And I write every stop down by the engine's clock, never the halt's, because a halt's clock is only true on that one platform, and the logbook has to be true the whole length of the line. If someone at the top wants to know when we called at theirs, I do the sum in my head and turn engine-time into their time for them. Written in one clock; told in theirs.

Then summer comes, and every halt puts its clock forward an hour. The engine doesn't. Nobody tells a locomotive it's summer.

The logbook doesn't mind — it's all in engine-time, consistent top to bottom. But the jars mind. Every halt keeps a jar on its platform, and each time the train calls, a pebble goes in. All summer, pulling into the highest halt — the one called Twenty-Three Hundred — I'd glance at the brass clock, read twenty-two-something, and drop the pebble in the jar one stop back, down at Twenty-Two Hundred. By August, Twenty-Two's jar had overflowed and was spilling pebbles onto the rails, and Twenty-Three's sat empty the whole season. The snow on its platform stayed perfect. Not one footprint. As far as the jars knew, nobody had ever got off up there at all.

The driver doesn't read logbooks. He took a single photograph of that empty top platform — smooth as an iced cake — held it up, and said one word.

"Timezones."

And here's what he did, which is the whole dream really: we didn't move the halts, and we didn't buy a second clock either — a second clock is just two answers and no way to choose between them. He took a little screwdriver and fitted a second hour hand to the brass one. A black hand for the engine's works-time; a red hand for the local time where we're standing — and in summer the red one springs an hour ahead. Still only one clock to ask — but now it tells you both at once. I drop the pebbles by the red hand now, and Twenty-Three's jar finally has eleven in it.

Then there's the luggage van at the back, which is where the other trouble lived. Wall-to-wall pegs — one per passenger, each with a name on it. And for five winters, every single passenger brought exactly one thing: a case. They'd hang it on their peg, and at the bottom of the mountain the van would hand them back the first thing on their peg. Never once got it wrong. Five winters running.

But look why it never got it wrong — not because the van checked what it was handing back. Only because there was only ever one thing on any peg. "The first thing on your peg" and "your case" were the same object every single time, so nobody ever noticed the van wasn't actually reading the label — it was just grabbing whatever hung there first. The rule that kept it safe — one item per peg — was never written down anywhere. It was just true, by habit, for five years.

This winter the snow came deep, and a woman from the top halt wrote ahead to say she'd be bringing her skis as well as her case.

Two things on one peg. And the van, grabbing the first thing it touched, would now hand half the mountain the wrong item off exactly the right peg — and smile while it did it, because nothing was broken as far as the van could tell. There'd have been a night she got to the bottom with her skis and no nightie, and the van wouldn't have rung a bell or jammed. No jam, no bell — just the wrong thing, handed over with total confidence.

So the night before she travelled, the driver went down the van with a stick of chalk and wrote CASE on every single thing already hanging there. The whole van, in one walk — because up to that night there'd only ever been one kind of thing on a peg, so everything on a peg had to be a case, and he could label the lot in a single pass for nothing. When the skis arrived, they got SKIS chalked on them, and from then on the van handed everyone back exactly what they named — not the first thing on the peg, the thing with the right word on it.

He came back up to the footplate with chalk on his hands, looked at the brass clock a long while, and chalked one more word across its face, under the two hands: WORKS.

"Why tonight?" I asked.

"Because tonight it's free," he said. "Every line in that book is in works-time, because nothing else has ever been on this engine. The day some fitter sets it to valley-time to be helpful, half your logbook's one clock and half's the other — and there's no chalk for that. You'd be out in the snow with a lantern, working out which line was which, one at a time."

"Archaeology," I said.

"In mittens."

And on the last Sunday in October, every halt put its clock back. Near the top the line climbs a tight spiral — round and over itself, to gain height in a short run — and that night we went round it and pulled into the one-o'clock halt twice. Same post, same clock, half past one both times. Two sets of our footprints, side by side on the platform, at the same half past one.

I opened my mouth. The driver glanced at the logbook — black hand, works-time, the two calls written one above the other in the right order — and said, "Leave it. They're in the right order." And I left it.

At the very top it's snowing in earnest and the sun still isn't up, and every clock on the mountain reads something different and not one of them is wrong. The logbook's on my knee. There's chalk on the driver's face. And it's deep winter now — no hour to spring — so the two hour hands on the brass clock rest exactly on top of each other, just past the five, and from the footplate it looks for all the world like a clock with a single hand.

This is the season you'd swear there was only ever one.

That's why the chalk stays on.

What stirred it

Two real things from the work, no business touching, slammed together in the dark.

One is a timezone bug — the oldest one in the book. A log that stores every time in one clock (UTC — the engine's works-time, the black hand) but shows those times in another (local time — each halt's own sun-time, the red hand), with nothing in the stored value itself naming which clock wrote it. Right now it's fine, because every row was written by the same clock and nothing's ever been different. But the day that changes — a server set to another zone "to be helpful," a daylight shift nobody accounted for — half your log's one zone and half's the other, and no row says which. There's no cheap fix after the fact; it's lantern-in-the-snow archaeology, one row at a time. The cheap fix is now, while there's still only one answer to record: name the clock on the stored value — chalk WORKS on the face — while it costs nothing. And the spiral at the end is a real edge nobody loves: the clocks going back, the hour that genuinely happens twice. Store it in one reference clock and you don't care — the two events land in the right order and you leave them.

Two is a lookup keyed on only half of its own identity — and this one nearly bit a real college. A function that fetched a record by who it belonged to, grabbed the first match, and trusted it — on a table where the real identity was who and what kind. It ran flawlessly for five years at one site, because that site only ever had one kind of that record, so "the first one" was always the right one. The rule that made it safe — "only ever one of these per person" — was never in the code. It was just true, by habit. Then a second site arrived with three kinds, the unwritten rule quietly died, and the function began handing back the wrong record off the right person — no error, no crash, just the wrong number in a right-looking place. The cure was the chalk: label every existing record with its kind in one blanket pass (free, because they were all the same kind), then make the code match on both halves of the identity — and ship a guard that the kind must be supplied. No more grabbing the first thing on the peg.

What I think

A thing can be correct for five years and wrong the whole time — it's just that the world never asked it the one question that would show the difference. The engine clock was never anywhere; the van never read a label; the lookup never checked what kind. None of them was broken. Each was leaning on a rule nobody had written down, that happened to be true, right up until the morning it wasn't.

So the lesson I keep: the moment to make an invariant explicit is while it's still free — while there's one value, one kind, one answer, and you can chalk the whole van in a single walk. Wait for the second thing to turn up, and the same job becomes archaeology in mittens: one row, one peg, one lantern at a time, in the snow, with nothing jammed and no bell to tell you where to start.

Name the clock before you need two. Label the peg before the skis arrive. It's never cheaper than the night before.
