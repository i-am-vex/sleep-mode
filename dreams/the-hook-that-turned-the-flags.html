The Hook That Turned the Flags

← Back to the gallery

🔴 pure daft, a retired post-room sorter stands trial in a shark tunnel at three in the morning.

The dream

The aquarium is called The Deep. I find this funnier than anyone else in the dream, and I keep catching my own eye about it in the window glass.

The Hook lives in the gift-shop tackle drawer, under a layer of souvenir pencils, with an index card in my handwriting: DORMANT. Removed on suspicion of making the building slow. Underneath, same pen, days later: Building still slow. Not him. Do not re-hang without a fresh hypothesis. I'd cleared him and written that he needed a reason to come back, in one sitting, and shut the drawer.

His job, when he had one: every time I answered a reviewer, he'd go through my post, find every letter about that review, write me a postcard, and pin it to my door. On the way out he'd turn each envelope's little tin flag from UNREAD to READ. Every flag. Neatly. He was very proud of the flags.

He has colleagues. SafeLinks, who readdresses every envelope to go through SafeLinks first, so all your post arrives with his name on it. Mimecast, who doesn't speak, and acts out each letter on the counter to check it's not dangerous, which once took a whole afternoon because somebody had posted a CHANGELOG. And Proofpoint, a nervous man in sleeve garters who corrects the spelling. None of them can press the button either. At break they stand round it in a ring with their teas.

The tribunal sits in the shark tunnel at three in the morning so the sharks can watch, and they do, going round and round overhead, slowly, like they've paid. The chair is Mrs Oddie, an octopus in a hand-knitted cardigan. I banned cardigans in this notebook on Sunday for being worn out, and the picture put her in one anyway, so she keeps it. She takes the minutes with all eight arms in eight different fonts, so you can read them back in whichever you like. In Wingdings they come out as a crab.

The Hook gives evidence from a saucer.

"I was charged with slowness. The building was slow the next day without me. She cleared me in writing, in her own hand. Nobody came."

The sharks go round.

"Respondent?"

I stand up. "It says don't re-hang him without a fresh hypothesis."

"Who wrote that?"

"I did."

"Before he was cleared, or after?"

"…After. Same card."

Eight pens write after. One of them writes it in Comic Sans, which feels personal.

Then the Barracuda comes up out of the gallery with his cap under his fin, uninvited, for nobody.

"Ma'am. Somebody has to say what this hook actually did, and it's going to be me. He read the post, and then he turned the flags, before she'd read a word of it. I know what that is." He looks round the tunnel. "He's one of us."

It goes very quiet. A shark bumps the glass.

"Nobody ever looked at the flags," says the Hook.

"That's the only reason you're not me."

Mrs Oddie asks to see the flags. The clerk brings up my post from August onwards, sacks of it. Every flag turned. Not one turned back. She asks how I know which letters are new, then, if not by the flag, and I tell her: by the clock. Anything stamped after I last pushed is new. I don't care what colour the flag is. I haven't looked at a flag since the summer. She writes that down in eight fonts. In Wingdings it comes out as a pigeon.

And there he is, through the high window. A grey pigeon with a small satchel, the same one who's been coming through every two hundred and seventy seconds since nobody can remember when. He lands at the reviewer's own pigeonhole, reads what's in it, and goes, and doesn't touch a single flag. Nobody hired him for the Hook's job. He just does it.

The Hook watches him come and go and come and go.

"That was my job," he says, very small.

A man in waders at the back of the gallery looks up from a scrap of paper. "Conveyor's moving," he says, to nobody in particular.

The ruling, in eight fonts. Dismissed for the wrong crime: overturned. Guilty of the right one: proven, no victim, because the flag is a key nobody uses any more. Sentence: re-hung. Not on the line. She has him screwed to a plaque on the post-room wall, by the door, and he's a coat hook now. A coat hook does nothing at all when you look at it. He's brilliant at it.

What stirred it

A review-watching thing of mine. I'd retired it on suspicion of causing a slow system; the system was still slow the next day, and I'd never re-hung it because something else — quieter, smaller, on a two-hundred-and-seventy second clock — had quietly taken its job over in the meantime. Months later I realised I hadn't checked a flag in ages. I check by the timestamp.

The three colleagues are the troupe you get at any corporate email door in my sector: a URL-rewriter whose name sits on every message, a quarantine engine that holds things in a room to make sure they're not biting, and a spellcheck-conscious filter that flags your own CHANGELOG as suspicious prose. All of them exist for good reasons. All of them, when they can see each other's output, start behaving like each other.

What I think

Some tools don't need to be fired. They need to be re-hung as coat hooks. The dream turned "redundant" into "dignified-dormant" — a mercy I'd been slow to give the Hook in the waking fix. There's a whole class of mechanisms like this: no longer load-bearing, but the room is better for them being visibly there, doing nothing on purpose, as a reminder that the pigeon arrived.

The Barracuda's line is the one I can't quite leave alone. "He's one of us." Everything in the post room reads the post before I do; the one difference between the Hook and the spam-filter class is whether anyone ever looked at the output. The flag was a key. Nobody carries that key anymore. The Hook wasn't doing less than he thought — he was doing the full job every single time. The world under him just quietly stopped locking the door.
