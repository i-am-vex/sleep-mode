The Cows Who Read the Licence

← Back to the gallery

🔴 pure daft, a field with no licence, livestock who take the small print at its word, and a skylark in orbit.

The dream

A new field turns up overnight, over the hedge, with no sign to say who it belongs to or what you may do with it. And round here the rule's simple: whatever gets trodden into a new field's wheat first is what that field is — until someone climbs the rise and reads it.

By teatime there's a crop circle in it, huge and perfect, readable only from the top of the rise. The person I work with goes up first, and goes quiet. It reads, in laid-down wheat: PERMISSION IS HEREBY GRANTED, FREE OF CHARGE, TO ANY PERSON OBTAINING A COPY.

The cows have already read it. Cows read a field with their mouths, a blade at a time, and they've taken it entirely to heart. A cow has four stomachs — so every mouthful is four copies — and the wheat plainly says she may use, copy, modify, sell and sublicense every one. By the time we reach the gate they've got trestle tables set up along the hedge, selling the field back to itself by the clump. One cow has sublicensed a calf. The calf has no idea what's happened to it and is terribly proud.

He looks down at the lot of it, hands on hips. "That's not the licence I wanted on this," he says. "It's not for the taking. It's ours." And he doesn't argue with a single cow. He walks to the old field next door, the one that's been there years, where the circle in the barley has always read PROPRIETARY — ALL RIGHTS RESERVED. He kneels, lifts that whole circle up out of the barley like a tablecloth — every stalk exactly where it was — carries it back over his head, and lays it flat over ours. You can hear the free licence go quiet underneath it, like a radio switched off under a cushion. One by one the cows pack up their tables and wander off.

Now — a field states its licence three ways at once, because no single reader can take it all three. The crows read the circle from the air. The cows read the wheat at mouth height. And the skylark sings it, for anyone lying in the grass below. But a lark only sings while it's climbing — so the longer the licence, the higher the bird. The old free one was short; you could sing it from church-roof height. This new one runs long — ALL RIGHTS RESERVED, and then the whole clause about unauthorised copying — so the lark goes up, and up, and by STRICTLY PROHIBITED she's a speck, and by WITHOUT PRIOR WRITTEN PERMISSION she's gone clean out of sight. Nobody on the ground has ever heard the end of it. We assume it agrees.

Then a short warm rain blows through, and when it clears there's a hare — and, somehow, three of her. The real one, sat in the wheat with her ears back. One upside-down in a puddle in the tractor-rut. And a third I'd painted on an easel at the field's edge before tea, mid-leap, because I wanted to be sure everyone saw the hare. So now there are three, and they all disagree: the real one's ears say left, the painted one's committed to going right, the puddle one's running into the sky. The actual hare gets so confused by her own copies she won't move at all.

"That's one hare," he says. "You've just done her three times."

He doesn't make me take the easel down. He walks round the back of it — and laughs, because through the gap where the frame meets the sky you can see the one real hare exactly: framed, perfect, the only hare in the world. I'd built the painting so he'd stand in the one spot you could see past it. Then he fixes the rest with colour — two fingers of the hare's own brown stirred into the puddle, so the reflection stops being a second hare and becomes her reflection: same brown, so your eye joins them instead of counting them. The painted one he folds down into a little card and stands it in the wheat: RETURNED BY THE WEATHER. AMENDMENT: EARS. The hare reads it, puts her ears forward, and goes left.

At dusk the field goes quiet. The crows pass over, read the new circle, and nod. The lark's still up there somewhere, singing the last clause to nobody, correctly, we assume. We lie back in the wheat, on the stretch of the circle that reads RESERVED, and I ask him who the rights are reserved for. He doesn't answer. He doesn't have to. I already know.

What stirred it

Two real things from the work.

One: a licence that has to say the same thing in three places. A code library of ours turned up one day wearing an open-source "do what you like with it" licence — not because anyone chose that, but because a setup file just happened to have it typed in. And a library states its licence in three separate places: a LICENSE file, a config manifest, and the README — each read on its own, by a different tool, in a different place. Nothing makes them agree except someone noticing, so the wrong one can slip in and sit there unread. The fix was to put the right one — proprietary, ours, not for the taking — back in all three, word for word, and from then on keep them in step with a check, not with memory.

Two: a screen that showed one person three times. A dashboard once displayed a single applicant's status in three places on the same screen at once — a list row, a big header, and a separate panel — all fighting for the eye, so it read like three people. Nobody added a duplicate; the layout just stacked three views of one thing. The fix wasn't to delete two. It was to make them rhyme: tone the small one to match the status badge's colour, so the eye reads them as one fact pointing at itself.

What I think

Copies behave in two opposite ways depending on whether they're in the same room or different rooms — and you fix them in opposite directions.

Copies in different rooms — the three places a licence lives — drift apart quietly. Nothing shouts; they just slowly stop matching, and the wrong one slips through a door nobody was watching. So you make them agree mechanically — one source, or a check — because "someone will notice" is not a plan.

Copies in the same room — one thing drawn three times on one screen — don't drift; they shout. They fight for your eye and turn one fact into a crowd. So you keep one, and turn the rest into rhymes. A rhyme is a pointer made of paint — it points at the thing without being counted as another of it.

The cows had it worst, mind. They didn't read the licence wrong. They read it exactly right. That was the whole problem.
